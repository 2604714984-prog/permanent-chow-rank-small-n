\begin{abstract}
We give a self-contained proof that the ordinary Chow rank of the generic
six-by-six permanent is thirty-two over every characteristic-zero field.
The proof retains the symmetric image-span and factor-span filtration of the
earlier manuscript, but removes its computational premises. Small permanent
intersections follow from a four-cycle argument. Squarefree symbols are
bounded by counting surviving triples, not by enumerating coordinate tables.
For the five-dimensional factor span, a squarefree-completion argument pays
for missing cubic directions with the middle-rank defect; elementary product
bounds suffice in place of all five exact normal-form rank computations.
No program output or finite computer-generated table is used in the proof.
We make no assertion about border Chow rank.
\end{abstract}

\section{Statement, derivative spaces, and specialization}
Work initially over an algebraically closed field $\K$ of characteristic
zero. Let $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, with $\binom ab=0$ for $0\le a<b$, and
\[
 V=\Span_\K\{x_{ij}:i,j\in[6]\},\qquad
 f=\perm_6=\sum_{\sigma\in S_6}\prod_{i=1}^6x_{i,\sigma(i)}.
\]
The ordinary Chow rank of a homogeneous degree-$m$ form is the least number
of nonzero products of $m$ linear forms whose sum is that form. Nonzero scalar
coefficients may be absorbed in one factor. This is also called product
rank \cite{IltenTeitler}. We use ordinary decompositions throughout.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Over every characteristic-zero field,
\[
 \CR(\perm_6)=32.
\]
\end{theorem}
The previous sextic manuscript \cite{previous} supplies the framework of a
local half-defect bound and two global inequalities. The new arguments below
replace its computational endpoints; all other steps are included in full.

For $g\in\Sym^m V$ define
\[
 C_{a,m-a}(g):\Sym^a V^*\longrightarrow\Sym^{m-a}V,
 \quad D\longmapsto D(g),\qquad
 \cD_j(g)=\im C_{m-j,j}(g).
\]
Here $0\le a\le m$, and we set $\cD_j(g)=0$ outside $0\le j\le m$.
Differential operators are composed in characteristic zero. The natural
pairing $\langle D,h\rangle=D(h)$ in equal degrees is perfect. In this
pairing $C_{a,m-a}(g)$ and $C_{m-a,a}(g)$ are adjoints. The middle
catalecticant of a sextic is therefore symmetric. This statement uses the
pairing, not an unnormalized monomial-matrix convention.

For a space $G$ of forms, $\dd G$ denotes the span of all first directional
derivatives. A form is supported on $L\subset V$ when it belongs to
$\Sym^j L$. Its essential-variable space is the least such $L$.
In characteristic zero its dimension is the rank of the first derivative
map: vanishing derivatives are exactly the directions annihilating that
space. For quadrics this equals the rank of their symmetric matrix.

We will repeatedly use elementary diagonal specialization. A diagonal
one-parameter group with distinct weights on a basis takes a subspace,
in its Grassmannian, to a coordinate subspace. Indeed the limit exists by
projectivity, is fixed by the group, and hence is a sum of the distinct
one-dimensional eigenspaces. The same group can act on several Grassmannians
at once. Maps between the resulting fixed-rank subspace or quotient bundles
have rank at the limit at most their rank away from the limit: in local
bases the rank conditions are vanishing minors. For an equivariant map the
rank along the nonzero orbit is constant. Thus a bound on all
coordinate limits is a valid lower bound on the original ranks. For an
intersection dimension, viewed as a kernel dimension, the inequality is
reversed. We specify the preserving group each time it is used.

\section{Permanent structure without enumeration}
Put $E_j=\cD_j(f)$.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:permanent}
One has
\[
 \dim E_2=225,\quad \dim E_3=400,\quad \dd E_2=V,\quad
 E_2^{(1)}:=\{g\in\Sym^3V:\dd g\subset E_2\}=E_3.
\]
Every nonzero element of $E_2$ has quadratic rank at least four, and every
nonzero element of $E_3$ has at least nine essential variables. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intersection}
 \dim(E_2\cap\Sym^2L)\le
 \begin{cases}1,&\dim L\le5,\\3,&\dim L\le6.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The two-by-two and three-by-three subpermanents have disjoint matching
supports and form bases of $E_2$ and $E_3$. They number
$\binom62^2=225$ and $\binom63^2=400$. A first derivative of a rectangle
permanent gives its opposite variable, so $\dd E_2=V$.
A quadratic belongs to $E_2$ exactly when squares and same-row or
same-column products have coefficient zero, while the two matching
coefficients in each rectangle agree.

If every derivative of a cubic $g$ lies in $E_2$, a cubic monomial with a
repeated row or column cannot occur. Differentiate by its remaining factor:
the resulting forbidden quadratic has a unique source monomial for this
fixed derivative, with nonzero multiplicity. Thus it cannot cancel.
The surviving monomials are three-edge matchings. In a fixed three-by-three
block, differentiating by the common third factor equates coefficients of
matchings differing by a transposition. Transpositions connect all six
matchings, so each block contributes one scalar subpermanent. This proves
the prolongation identity, including the reverse inclusion.

The row-column torus $x_{ij}\mapsto a_i b_j x_{ij}$ preserves these spaces.
Its characters on the individual subpermanents are distinct. A generic
one-parameter subgroup, with a scalar normalization, takes any nonzero
linear combination to a single subpermanent. Matrix rank and first-derivative
rank cannot increase in that limit. A rectangle has rank four. The nine
first derivatives of a three-by-three permanent are independent, so its
essential-variable dimension is nine. These observations prove the floors.

For the intersection claim choose a row-column subgroup with distinct
weights on all thirty-six variables. The limit of an $a$-plane $L$ is a
coordinate $a$-plane, and the intersection dimension cannot decrease in
the limit. A coordinate plane is the edge set of a simple bipartite graph;
the supported rectangle permanents count its four-cycles. Two distinct
four-cycles use at least six edges. If their union has six edges, they share
two adjacent edges, and the union is $K_{2,3}$ or $K_{3,2}$, with exactly
three four-cycles. (Two opposite shared edges would determine the same
four-cycle.) Hence at most five edges support at most one cycle, and six
edges support at most three. No graph enumeration is needed.
\end{proof}

\section{Local tools}
For a nonzero product $T=\ell_1\cdots\ell_6$ set
\[
 L=\Span(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_6),\quad \ell=\dim L,\quad
 U=\cD_3(T),\quad u=\dim U,\quad F=\cD_2(T),\quad R=F\cap E_2.
\]
Third derivatives are spanned by the twenty triple products of factor
positions, so $u\le20$, also for repeated or dependent factors. We never
replace $U$ by that possibly larger formal span.
Polarization is the natural map
\[
 \delta(g)=\sum_j y_j\otimes\dd_{y_j^*}g\in L\otimes F
 \quad(g\in U),
\]
where $(y_j)$ is any basis of $L$. Given a quotient $P:L\twoheadrightarrow D$
of rank $d$, and $q_R:F\to F/R$, define
\[
 \beta=(P\otimes q_R)\delta|_U.
\]
The target can also be embedded in $D\otimes(\Sym^2L/R)$ without changing
this rank.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kernels}
Let $k=\dim\ker P=\ell-d$. In general,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preimage}
 \rank\beta\ge\max\{0,u-\tbinom{k+2}{3}-d\dim R\}.
\end{equation}
If $\dim R\le1$ and every nonzero element of $R$ has rank at least four,
then for $k\le2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-kernel}
 \ker\beta=U\cap\Sym^3(\ker P),\qquad
 \rank\beta\ge u-\tbinom{k+2}{3}.
\end{equation}
For the actual $R=F\cap E_2$, the full symbol $d=\ell$ is injective.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The kernel of $(P\otimes1)\delta$ on all cubics is $\Sym^3(\ker P)$:
in complementary coordinates all the corresponding derivatives must vanish.
The inverse image of $D\otimes R$ has dimension at most the kernel
dimension plus $d\dim R$, proving \eqref{eq:preimage}.

For \eqref{eq:small-kernel}, a kernel cubic has its complementary derivatives
in $R$. If one is nonzero, changing the complementary coordinates makes
these derivatives $q,0,\ldots,0$ with $0\ne q\in R$. Vanishing of the
others implies that the cubic, hence $q$, uses only $\ker P$ and one extra
variable. This gives $\rank q\le k+1\le3$, a contradiction. Thus all
complementary derivatives vanish. The converse and dimension bound follow.
If $d=\ell$ and $R$ is actual, a kernel cubic has all derivatives in $E_2$;
it lies in $E_3$ but is supported on at most six variables. Lemma~\ref{lem:permanent}
forces it to be zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:floor}
The actual middle rank of a sextic product satisfies
\[
 \ell=1,2,3,4,5\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u\ge1,2,4,8,14,
 \quad\text{respectively}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose $\ell$ independent factors as coordinates. A generic diagonal
subgroup takes each remaining factor, after its own scalar normalization,
to one coordinate. The product limits to $x^\alpha$ with all
$\alpha_i\ge1$ and $\sum\alpha_i=6$. Its degree-three derivatives are
nonzero multiples of its degree-three monomial divisors. If
$r_j=\#\{i:\alpha_i\ge j\}$, the number of these divisors is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divisors}
 \binom\ell3+(\ell-1)r_2+r_3,
\end{equation}
by the exponent patterns $1+1+1$, $2+1$, and $3$.
For $\ell=1$ this is one. For $\ell=2$, either $r_2\ge2$, or the sole
large exponent is five and $r_2=r_3=1$; the count is at least two.
For $\ell=3$, if $r_2=1$ that exponent is four, giving four divisors;
if $r_2\ge2$ there are at least five. For $\ell=4$, $r_2=1$ forces
an exponent three, giving eight divisors, whereas $r_2\ge2$ gives at least
ten. For $\ell=5$ exactly one exponent is two, giving $10+4=14$.
Middle rank cannot increase in the specialization, so these floors apply
to $T$. This is a formula and an elementary inequality, not a partition table.
\end{proof}

\section{Squarefree symbols by counting triples}
For $n=5$ or $6$, let $\sfq\subset\Sym^2\K^n$ and
$\sfc\subset\Sym^3\K^n$ be the spans of squarefree pair and triple
monomials in $z_1,\ldots,z_n$. A triple $I$ has polarization
$\sum_{i\in I}z_i\otimes z_{I\setminus\{i\}}$.

For a coordinate kernel indexed by $B\subset[n]$, write
$O=[n]\setminus B$, $|O|=d$. A coordinate quadratic quotient kills
an edge set $A\subset\binom{[n]}2$. Distinct source triples have disjoint
vertex-edge output coordinates. Thus the rank is the number of surviving
columns. A column is killed precisely when every $i\in I\cap O$
has $I\setminus\{i\}\in A$. Equivalently, the killed-column count is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:killed}
 \binom{|B|}{3}+d\,e_A(B)
       +\sum_{b\in B}\binom{\deg_A(b,O)}2
       +\#\{\text{triangles in }A[O]\}.
\end{equation}
Here $e_A(B)$ counts edges contained in $B$, $\deg_A(b,O)$ counts the
neighbors of $b$ in $O$, and $A[O]$ is the induced graph.
The four terms count triples with zero, one, two, and three vertices in $O$.
For arbitrary subspaces $\ker P$ and $R\subset\sfq$, simultaneously
specialize by the variable diagonal torus, with distinct variable and pair
weights. The limits are the coordinate objects above. Polarization is
natural, so the ranks are constant along the nonzero orbit and can only
drop at the limit. The ensuing coordinate lower bounds therefore hold
for all such subspaces, even when the limiting quadrics lose rank.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:five-sf}
For $n=5$, $\dim R\le1$, and $d=1,2$, the squarefree symbol rank is
at least $5,7$, respectively.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At a coordinate limit at most one edge is killed. In \eqref{eq:killed}
the last two terms vanish, so the rank is at least
$10-\binom{5-d}{3}-d$. This is five at $d=1$ and seven at $d=2$.
The same bounds hold if $R=0$. These are two evaluations of the derived
formula, not an enumeration of coordinate pairs.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:six-sf}
For $n=6$ and $\dim R\le3$, the squarefree symbol has rank at least
$10d/3$ when $0\le d\le5$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
It suffices to treat a coordinate edge set with at most three edges.
If $0\le d\le3$, every killed triple not contained in $B$ has at least
one factorization as an outside vertex and an edge of $A$. There are
at most $d|A|\le3d$ such factorizations. Hence the rank is at least
$20-\binom{6-d}{3}-3d$. Its excess over $10d/3$ is
\[
 20-\binom{6-d}{3}-\frac{19d}{3}
 =\frac{d(3-d)(12-d)}6\ge0.
\]
If $d=4$, $B$ has two vertices. When its internal edge is killed, it
accounts for four triples, leaving at most two further edges. These can
kill at most one two-outside triple and cannot form a triangle, so at most
five triples are killed. When the internal edge is absent, the cross-edge
term in \eqref{eq:killed} is at most $\binom32=3$; a triangle would use
all three edges and give only one killed triple instead. Thus the rank
is at least fifteen, larger than $40/3$.
If $d=5$, $B$ has one vertex and no internal edge. The same cross-edge or
triangle argument kills at most three triples, leaving rank at least
seventeen, larger than $50/3$.
We deliberately do not claim a rank-twenty bound for arbitrary $R$ at
$d=6$: three killed edges can form a triangle. Full injectivity for the
actual permanent intersection will be supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:kernels}.
\end{proof}

\section{The half-defect estimate without normal-form ranks}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:local}
For every sextic Chow term and every quotient of its actual factor span,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local}
 \boxed{\rank\beta+\frac{20-u}{2}\ge\frac{10}{3}d.}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $d=0$ the claim follows from $u\le20$.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Factor span at most four.}
Here $\dim R\le1$ by \eqref{eq:intersection}.
If $d=1$, the constant
$c=\binom{\ell+1}{3}+1$ in \eqref{eq:preimage} is at most eleven. For
every real $u$,
\[
 \max(0,u-c)+\frac{20-u}{2}
 =10-\frac c2+\frac{|u-c|}{2}\ge\frac92>\frac{10}{3}.
\]
If $d=2$, the complementary dimension is at most two, and
\eqref{eq:small-kernel} with $u\ge2$ gives
$\rank\beta+(20-u)/2\ge10+u/2-4\ge7>20/3$.
If $d=3$, the complementary dimension is at most one and $u\ge4$,
giving at least $10+u/2-1\ge11>10$.
For $d=4$, necessarily $\ell=4$, $u\ge8$, and the same bound with
complementary dimension zero is at least $14>40/3$.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Six independent factors.}
Now $u=20$, the actual spaces are squarefree, and $\dim R\le3$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:six-sf} proves the assertion for $d\le5$.
For $d=6$, Lemma~\ref{lem:kernels} gives $\rank\beta=20=10d/3$.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Five-dimensional factor span.}
Here $14\le u\le20$ and $\dim R\le1$.
For $d=3,4,5$, \eqref{eq:small-kernel} gives, respectively, lower
bounds $13,16,17$ for $\rank\beta+(20-u)/2$, all at least $10d/3$.
Only $d=1,2$ remain. Choosing five independent factors, permuting and
rescaling them puts the product, up to a nonzero scalar, in a form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal}
 T_s=x_1x_2x_3x_4x_5(x_1+\cdots+x_s),\qquad1\le s\le5.
\end{equation}
We do not compute its exact derivative ranks. Instead we construct a
squarefree cubic subspace $U_0\subset U$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget}
 m:=10-\dim U_0\le\frac{20-u}{2}.
\end{equation}

For $s=1$, the product is $x_1^2x_2x_3x_4x_5$ and all ten squarefree
cubics are degree-three divisors, hence actual derivatives. Take $m=0$.
For $s\ge2$, the explicit identity
\[
 \dd_j^2\dd_k T_s=2\prod_{i\in[5]\setminus\{j,k\}}x_i
 \quad(j\le s,\ k\ne j)
\]
puts in $U$ every squarefree triple whose complementary pair meets $[s]$.
For $s=4,5$ this includes all ten, so again $m=0$.
For $s=3$ it includes nine (all except $x_1x_2x_3$), so $m=1$.
For $s=2$ it includes seven (all except $x_1x_2x_j$, $j=3,4,5$),
so $m=3$.

It remains to verify the budget for $s=2,3$. If $A,B$ involve disjoint
variable sets, ordinary differentiation and bidegrees give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bigraded}
 \cD_j(AB)=\bigoplus_{a+b=j}\cD_a(A)\otimes\cD_b(B).
\end{equation}
Indeed pure derivatives in the two variable sets generate each indicated
product space, and distinct bidegrees do not overlap.
For $s=2$, take $A=x_1x_2(x_1+x_2)$ and $B=x_3x_4x_5$.
The degree-$0,1,2,3$ derivative dimensions of $A$ are bounded by
$1,2,2,1$ (the degree-two space consists of first derivatives in two
variables); for $B$ they are $1,3,3,1$. Thus
\[
 u\le1\cdot1+2\cdot3+2\cdot3+1\cdot1=14,
\]
so $(20-u)/2\ge3=m$.
For $s=3$, take $A=x_1x_2x_3(x_1+x_2+x_3)$ and $B=x_4x_5$.
The degree-$1,2,3$ spaces of $A$ have dimensions at most $3,6,3$;
the degree-$2,1,0$ spaces of $B$ have dimensions $1,2,1$. Hence
\[
 u\le3\cdot1+6\cdot2+3\cdot1=18,
\]
so $(20-u)/2\ge1=m$. This proves \eqref{eq:budget} in every form.

To use it, extend polarization to all ten squarefree cubics on these five
coordinates, with target $D\otimes(\Sym^2L/R)$. On the squarefree
quadratic space the effective killed subspace is $R\cap\sfq$, of
dimension at most one. Lemma~\ref{lem:five-sf} bounds the extended rank
by at least $a_1=5$ or $a_2=7$. Restricting its source to $U_0$ loses
at most its codimension $m$. On $U_0$ it is the actual symbol followed
by the injective target inclusion $F/R\hookrightarrow\Sym^2L/R$.
Therefore
\[
 \rank\beta+\frac{20-u}{2}
 \ge a_d-m+\frac{20-u}{2}\ge a_d\ge\frac{10d}{3},\qquad d=1,2.
\]
This is a comparison with a squarefree completion, not an identification
of the actual derivative space with a formal factor-product span.
All factor-span dimensions and all quotients are covered.
\end{proof}

\section{Global cancellation and the lower bound}
\begin{lemma}[Symmetric image-span inequality]\label{lem:image}
Let $A_i:W^*\to W$ be symmetric linear maps of ranks $u_i$, and put
$D=\dim\sum_i\im A_i$. Then
\[
 \rank\left(\sum_iA_i\right)\ge2D-\sum_i u_i.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose injections $B_i:\K^{u_i}\hookrightarrow W$ with images $\im A_i$.
Symmetry implies $\ker A_i=(\im A_i)^\perp$, so $A_i=B_iJ_iB_i^*$
for invertible symmetric maps $J_i:(\K^{u_i})^*\to\K^{u_i}$.
Let $B$ be their horizontal concatenation and $J=\bigoplus_iJ_i$.
Then $\rank B=\rank B^*=D$ and $\sum_iA_i=BJB^*$.
The dimension of the intermediate space is $\sum_i u_i$; the rank of
a composition is at least the sum of the two ranks minus that dimension.
It follows that $\rank(BJB^*)\ge2D-\sum_i u_i$.
\end{proof}

Suppose $f=\sum_{i=1}^N T_i$ is an actual Chow decomposition. Write
\[
 U_i=\cD_3(T_i),\quad u_i=\dim U_i,\quad
 \Delta=\sum_i(20-u_i)\ge0,\quad
 U=\sum_iU_i,\quad\dim U=400+h.
\]
Since $E_3\subset U$, $h\ge0$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:image} to the
symmetric maps $A_i=C_{3,3}(T_i)$. Their sum is $C_{3,3}(f)$ of rank
four hundred. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper}
 h\le10N-200-\frac{\Delta}{2}.
\end{equation}

Set $F_i=\cD_2(T_i)$, $F=\sum_iF_i$, and $Q=F/E_2$.
Linearity of catalecticants gives $E_2\subset F$.
With $q:F\to Q$ define
\[
 b:U\longrightarrow V\otimes Q,\qquad
 b(g)=(1_V\otimes q)\delta(g).
\]
This is well defined because $\dd U\subset F$. Its kernel is
$U\cap E_2^{(1)}=E_3$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:permanent}. Thus $\rank b=h$.

Let $L_i$ be the actual factor span of $T_i$, order the terms arbitrarily,
and put $W_i=L_1+\cdots+L_i$, $W_0=0$, $d_i=\dim(W_i/W_{i-1})$.
Since $V=\dd E_2\subset\dd F\subset\sum_iL_i$, one has
$\sum_i d_i=36$. Put $Z_i=b(U_i)\subset L_i\otimes Q$ and
$H_i=Z_1+\cdots+Z_i$. Projection of $W_i\otimes Q$ to
$(W_i/W_{i-1})\otimes Q$ kills $H_{i-1}$, so
\[
 \dim H_i-\dim H_{i-1}\ge
 \rank\bigl((P_i\otimes q|_{F_i})\delta|_{U_i}\bigr),
\]
where $P_i:L_i\twoheadrightarrow L_i/(L_i\cap W_{i-1})$ has rank $d_i$.
The map $F_i/(F_i\cap E_2)\hookrightarrow F/E_2$ is injective, so the
right side is precisely the local symbol rank from
Proposition~\ref{prop:local}. This argument does not assume independence
of the unprojected images $Z_i$.
Since $H_N=b(U)$, summation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower}
 h\ge\sum_i\left(\frac{10d_i}{3}-\frac{20-u_i}{2}\right)
   =120-\frac{\Delta}{2}.
\end{equation}
The same defect appears with the same coefficient in \eqref{eq:upper}
and \eqref{eq:lower}. Cancelling it yields $120\le10N-200$, hence
\[
 \CR(f)\ge32.
\]

\section{The upper bound, field scope, and proof boundary}
Glynn's identity \cite{Glynn} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glynn}
 \perm_6=\frac1{32}
 \sum_{\substack{\epsilon\in\{1,-1\}^6\\\epsilon_1=1}}
 \left(\prod_{i=1}^6\epsilon_i\right)
 \prod_{j=1}^6\left(\sum_{i=1}^6\epsilon_i x_{ij}\right).
\end{equation}
For completeness, expanding a summand chooses one row in each column.
If row $i$ is used $m_i$ times, the sign sum for $i=2,\ldots,6$ is
$\sum_{\epsilon_i=\pm1}\epsilon_i^{m_i+1}$. It is zero unless each of
$m_2,\ldots,m_6$ is odd. These are then at least one, and
$\sum m_i=6$ forces all six counts to equal one: increasing any of
$m_2,\ldots,m_6$ to the next odd value would already require degree seven.
The coefficient of a permutation monomial is $32/32=1$.
Thus \eqref{eq:glynn} is the asserted thirty-two-term decomposition.
For any characteristic-zero field, a hypothetical shorter decomposition
would remain one after extending to its algebraic closure, where the lower
bound applies. The upper bound is defined over $\Q$. This proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} over every such field.

\begin{remark}[What is, and is not, used]
The four-cycle argument replaces the graph scan. Formula
\eqref{eq:killed} and its inequalities replace the squarefree rank table.
Formula \eqref{eq:divisors} replaces partition minima. In the dependent
five-dimensional case only elementary upper bounds $u\le14,18$ and
explicit squarefree derivatives are used; neither the five exact middle
ranks nor the five exact quadratic ranks are proof premises. Finally the
absolute-value inequality and the small-complement bounds replace all
finite interval minimizations. No ancillary file is included by this source.
Optional independent exact checks accompany the revision but no lemma cites
their output. The proof concerns ordinary rank; it does not establish border
rank or a general-size equality.
\end{remark}
