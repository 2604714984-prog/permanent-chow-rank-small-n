\begin{abstract}
We prove that the ordinary Chow rank of the permanent of a generic
five-by-five matrix is sixteen over every field of characteristic zero.
The proof combines a rectangular image-span inequality with a local
polarization estimate for quintic products. A closed count of squarefree
triples, a uniform monomial-divisor bound, and explicit derivatives in
four variables establish the local estimate. No computer-generated rank,
finite search, or stored certificate is a premise. In particular, the
four normal-form catalecticant ranks need not be determined separately.
The argument does not assert an equality for border Chow rank.
\end{abstract}

\section{Statement and specialization conventions}
Let \(\K\) initially be algebraically closed of characteristic zero. Write
\([m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), and set
\[
 V=\Span_{\K}\{x_{ij}:i,j\in[5]\},\qquad
 f=\perm_5=\sum_{\sigma\in S_5}\prod_{i=1}^5x_{i,\sigma(i)}.
\]
Thus \(\dim V=25\), and elements of \(V\) are linear forms on \(V^*\).
The ordinary Chow rank, also called product rank, of a degree-\(m\) form
is the least number of nonzero products of \(m\) linear forms summing to
it; scalar coefficients may be absorbed into a factor.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every field \(\K\) of characteristic zero,
\[
 \CR_{\K}(\perm_5)=16.
\]
\end{theorem}
The factors in a Chow term may involve all twenty-five variables. This
is distinct from the tensor rank of the permanent in five separate row
spaces. Han and Song prove that the latter rank is sixteen
\cite{HanSong2026}; that lower bound is not used here. In fact, the
component with degree one in each row of the single Chow term
\(\prod_{j=1}^5(\sum_{i=1}^5x_{ij})\) is the permanent itself.
For background on product rank, see \cite{IltenTeitler2016}.

For a form \(h\in\Sym^mV\) let
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 C_{a,m-a}(h):\Sym^aV^*\longrightarrow\Sym^{m-a}V,
 \quad D\longmapsto D(h),\\
 \cD_j(h)=\im C_{m-j,j}(h).
 \end{gathered}
\]
All derivative spaces below are \emph{actual} images of these maps,
not formal spans of factor products. For a space of forms \(G\),
\(\dd G\) denotes the span of all first directional derivatives.
The differential pairing between symmetric powers is perfect in
characteristic zero. Under it \(C_{a,m-a}(h)^*=C_{m-a,a}(h)\), since
\(D'D(h)=DD'(h)\). In particular these maps have the same rank.

We record precisely how specialization is used. For a polynomial matrix,
rank at a special parameter value is no larger than rank for general
values: every minor that vanishes identically still vanishes at the
special value. Subspaces of a fixed dimension have limits under a diagonal
one-parameter action. One can see existence by taking the least-weight
nonzero term in their Pl\"ucker coordinates, after removing a common power
of the parameter. The limit is fixed by the action. When the weights on
the underlying basis vectors are distinct, every fixed subspace is a span
of basis vectors, so the limit is coordinate. The same argument applies
to several subspaces simultaneously. Locally on a Grassmannian, bases of
the subspaces and their quotients give regular matrices; the minor
criterion therefore applies to maps between these quotients as well.
Thus an intersection or a kernel dimension can increase, but cannot
decrease, at a special point. Only these elementary facts about limits
are required below; no classification of algebraic-group orbits is used.

\section{The permanent's derivative spaces}
Put \(E_j=\cD_j(f)\).
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:permanent}
One has
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \dim E_2=\dim E_3=100,\qquad \dd E_2=V,\\
 E_2^{(1)}:=\{g\in\Sym^3V:\dd g\subset E_2\}=E_3.
 \end{gathered}
\]
Every nonzero quadratic in \(E_2\) has matrix rank at least four. For
any \(L\subset V\) of dimension at most five,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intersection}
 \dim(E_2\cap\Sym^2L)\le1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The quadratic and cubic subpermanents form bases of \(E_2\) and \(E_3\).
Different row-set/column-set pairs have disjoint matching-monomial supports,
and their numbers are \(\binom52^2=\binom53^2=100\).
In particular,
\[
 E_2=\Span\{x_{ia}x_{jb}+x_{ib}x_{ja}:i<j,\ a<b\}.
\]
Differentiating a rectangle at one corner gives its opposite variable,
so \(\dd E_2=V\).

Suppose every first derivative of a cubic \(g\) belongs to \(E_2\).
A cubic monomial with two factors in one row or column, including a
repeated factor, is impossible: differentiation by the remaining factor
produces a quadratic monomial forbidden in \(E_2\). For a fixed
partial derivative and a fixed resulting monomial there is a unique
source monomial, so its coefficient cannot be cancelled by a different
source. Its derivative multiplicity is nonzero. Every surviving monomial
is consequently a three-edge matching. For fixed three rows and three
columns, swapping the assignments of two rows leaves the third factor
unchanged. Differentiating by that common factor forces the two matching
coefficients to agree. Transpositions connect all six permutations,
so their coefficients all agree. This proves \(E_2^{(1)}=E_3\), including
the immediate reverse inclusion.

For the remaining assertions use the row-column scaling
\begin{equation}\label{eq:row-column}
 \tau_t(x_{ij})=t^{r_i+c_j}x_{ij},\qquad
 r_i=2^{i-1},\quad c_j=2^{5+j-1},\quad t\ne0.
\end{equation}
The twenty-five variable weights are distinct. Both monomials of each
rectangle acquire the same weight, and the one hundred rectangle weights
are distinct, by uniqueness of binary expansion. Thus \(\tau_t\)
preserves \(E_2\), and any nonzero \(q\in E_2\) has a projectively
normalized limit equal to a nonzero rectangle. Invertible substitutions
and nonzero scalar multiplication preserve quadratic rank; specialization
cannot increase it. A rectangle is the sum of two products on four
independent variables, with a nonsingular four-dimensional symmetric
matrix. Its rank is four, proving the minimum-rank claim.

For \(a=\dim L\le5\), take the coordinate limit \(L_0\) of
\(\tau_tL\) in \(\operatorname{Gr}(a,V)\). The intersection in
\eqref{eq:intersection} is the kernel of
\(\Sym^2L\to\Sym^2V/E_2\). Because \(E_2\) is preserved and kernel
dimension cannot decrease at the limit, it suffices to bound the
intersection for \(L_0\). Let \(\Omega\subset[5]\times[5]\) be its
set of \(a\) variables. Distinct rectangles have disjoint quadratic
monomial supports, so \(E_2\cap\Sym^2L_0\) has as a basis precisely
the rectangles whose four corners lie in \(\Omega\).
Two different rectangles share at most two corners: three corners
determine the fourth. They therefore require at least six variables.
Since \(|\Omega|\le5\), at most one rectangle occurs.
\end{proof}

\section{Local polarization of a quintic product}
Let \(T=\ell_1\cdots\ell_5\ne0\), and put
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 L=\Span(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_5),\quad \ell=\dim L,\\
 F=\cD_2(T),\quad U=\cD_3(T),\quad u=\dim F=\dim U\le10.
 \end{gathered}
\]
The equality uses the differential pairing. The upper bound follows
because third derivatives are linear combinations of the ten pair
products of the five factors. Also \(F\subset\Sym^2L\) and
\(\dd U\subset F\). Let \(R=F\cap E_2\); by
Lemma~\ref{lem:permanent}, \(\dim R\le1\), and every nonzero element
of \(R\) has quadratic rank at least four.

For a surjection \(P:L\to D\), set \(d=\dim D\),
\(S=\ker P\), and \(k=\dim S=\ell-d\). Define
\[
 \delta:U\to L\otimes F,\qquad
 \delta(g)=\sum_j y_j\otimes\dd_{y_j^*}g,
 \qquad \beta=(P\otimes q_R)\delta,
\]
where \(q_R:F\to F/R\) is the quotient map. The displayed
polarization is independent of the choice of basis \((y_j)\) of \(L\).
It is injective, since multiplication of its two factors sends
\(\delta(g)\) to \(3g\).

\begin{lemma}[A small complementary span]\label{lem:kernel}
If \(k\le2\), then
\[
 \ker\beta=U\cap\Sym^3S,\qquad
 \rank\beta\ge u-\binom{k+2}{3}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a basis of \(S\), extended by complementary variables
\(z_1,\ldots,z_d\) in \(L\). For a kernel cubic the derivatives
\(\dd_{z_j^*}g\) all lie in \(R\). If any is nonzero, a change of
complementary coordinates makes these derivatives \(q,0,\ldots,0\)
for some \(0\ne q\in R\), since \(\dim R\le1\). Vanishing of the
last \(d-1\) derivatives implies that \(g\), and then \(q\), uses
only \(S\) and \(z_1\). Hence \(\rank q\le k+1\le3\), a
contradiction. All complementary derivatives must vanish, which is
exactly \(g\in\Sym^3S\). The converse is immediate, and
\(\dim\Sym^3S=\binom{k+2}{3}\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A uniform divisor bound]\label{lem:floor}
If \(1\le\ell\le4\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:floor}
 u\ge\binom{\ell}{2}+1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose \(\ell\) independent factors as coordinates \(y_1,\ldots,y_\ell\).
Scale the coordinates with distinct weights and divide each factor by
its own least power of the parameter. Each normalized factor tends to a
nonzero coordinate multiple. For nonzero parameter their product is a
scalar multiple of an invertible transform of \(T\). The limit is a
nonzero monomial \(c\prod_i y_i^{\alpha_i}\), with \(\alpha_i\ge1\) and
\(\sum_i\alpha_i=5\); the initially chosen coordinate factors ensure
that none of the \(\ell\) variables is lost. Its nonzero second
partials are indexed by its distinct degree-two divisors and yield
distinct cubic monomials. The number of these divisors is
\[
 \binom{\ell}{2}+\#\{i:\alpha_i\ge2\}.
\]
Since \(5>\ell\), at least one exponent is at least two. Rank of
the catalecticant cannot increase under specialization, giving
\eqref{eq:floor}. No enumeration of positive partitions is involved.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Five independent factors]\label{lem:independent}
If \(\ell=5\), then \(u=10\) and \(\rank\beta\ge2d\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the five factors as coordinates \(z_1,\ldots,z_5\). The spaces
\(U\) and \(F\) are respectively the squarefree cubic and quadratic
spaces. We prove the estimate for every subspace \(R\subset F\) of
dimension at most one, without its minimum-rank condition.

Replace \(P\) by the canonical quotient \(L\to L/S\), which does
not change the rank. Simultaneously specialize \(S\) and \(R\) by the
diagonal action with variable weights \(2^{i-1}\). The weights on
variables and on squarefree quadratic monomials are distinct. Both limits
are coordinate subspaces, and \(U,F\) are preserved throughout. By
polarization equivariance the rank is constant for \(t\ne0\), and
it cannot increase at the limit. For clarity, the kernel of the induced
quotient map on \(L\otimes F\) is
\(S\otimes F+L\otimes R\), of constant dimension
\(10k+5\dim R-k\dim R\). The specialization comparison is therefore
between fixed-rank subspace quotients, not between arbitrarily chosen
bases of changing dimension. The limit of \(R\) need not retain
quadratic rank four; our stronger statement does not require that.

Write \(z_J=\prod_{j\in J}z_j\). At the limit let \(B\subset[5]\) index the basis of \(S\), and set
\(b=|B|=5-d\). If \(R\ne0\), let \(e\in\binom{[5]}2\) be
the edge with \(R=\Span(z_e)\). A source triple \(I\in\binom{[5]}3\)
has components \(z_i\otimes z_{I\setminus\{i\}}\), for \(i\in I\).
A component survives exactly when \(i\notin B\) and its complementary
edge is not \(e\). Different triples have disjoint component supports,
so the rank is the number of surviving columns. A column is zero if
\(I\subset B\), or if it has exactly one vertex outside \(B\) and
its complementary edge is \(e\subset B\). A triple with two or more
vertices outside \(B\) cannot be killed by a single edge.
Consequently the coordinate rank is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triple-count}
 10-\binom b3-d\,\mathbf1_{\{R\ne0,\ e\subset B\}}.
\end{equation}
If \(b=0,1\), the indicator and binomial coefficient vanish, so this
rank is ten, at least \(2d\). If \(2\le b\le5\), subtracting
\(2d=2(5-b)\) from its lower bound gives
\[
 10-\binom b3-(5-b)-2(5-b)
 =\frac{(5-b)(b^2+2b-6)}6\ge0.
\]
This proves the assertion without a coordinate-case table.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[One new direction in a four-dimensional factor span]
\label{lem:four}
If \(\ell=4\) and \(d=1\), then
\[
 \rank\beta+\frac{10-u}{2}\ge2.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose four independent factors as coordinates. Up to a nonzero scalar,
rescaling and permuting them makes the product
\[
 T_s=x_1x_2x_3x_4(x_1+\cdots+x_s),\qquad 1\le s\le4.
\]
Identify the one-dimensional target of \(P\) with \(\K\). Then
\(\beta\) is differentiation in a nonzero direction
\(\lambda=\sum_{j=1}^4a_j\dd_{x_j}\), followed by the quotient by \(R\).
Write \(H_{ij}=\dd_{x_i}\dd_{x_j}T_s\).

For \(s=1\), \(H_{22}=H_{33}=H_{44}=0\), so \(u\le7\).
For \(s=2\), one has
\[
 H_{33}=H_{44}=0,\quad
 H_{11}=2x_2x_3x_4,\quad H_{22}=2x_1x_3x_4,\quad
 H_{12}=H_{11}+H_{22},
\]
so again \(u\le7\). In both cases \(U\) contains
\(g_1=x_1x_3x_4\) and \(g_2=x_2x_3x_4\): for \(s=1\) use
\(H_{12}/2,H_{11}/2\), and for \(s=2\) use \(H_{22}/2,H_{11}/2\).
Their supports together contain all four variables. Directly,
\(\dd_\lambda g_1=\dd_\lambda g_2=0\) forces all four \(a_j\) to
vanish, by comparing their distinct pair monomials. Thus at least one
of these derivatives is nonzero. It uses at most three variables and
has quadratic rank at most three, so it cannot lie in \(R\).
It follows that \(\rank\beta\ge1\), and
\(\rank\beta+(10-u)/2\ge1+3/2>2\).

For \(s=3\), the identities
\(H_{jj}=2\prod_{i\ne j}x_i\), for \(j=1,2,3\), show that \(U\)
contains \(x_1x_2x_4,x_1x_3x_4,x_2x_3x_4\).
If \(a_4\ne0\), their derivatives modulo \(x_4L\) are nonzero
multiples of three distinct pair monomials. Their derivative span has
dimension at least three, so its quotient by the at-most-one-dimensional
\(R\) has dimension at least two. If \(a_4=0\), relabel
\(x_1,x_2,x_3\) so that \(a_1\ne0\). The derivatives of
\(x_1x_2x_4,x_1x_3x_4\) are
\[
 x_4(a_1x_2+a_2x_1),\qquad x_4(a_1x_3+a_3x_1).
\]
They are independent. Every quadratic in their span is divisible by
\(x_4\), hence has rank at most two, so the span meets \(R\) trivially.
Again \(\rank\beta\ge2\).

For \(s=4\), the same diagonal derivative identities show that \(U\)
contains all four squarefree cubics. Choose \(a_j\ne0\). Differentiating
the three cubics containing \(x_j\), and reducing modulo \(x_jL\),
gives three independent pair monomials on the other three variables.
Quotienting their derivative span by \(R\) leaves dimension at least two.
For \(s=3,4\), \(u\le10\) now proves the asserted inequality.
In particular, neither of their exact catalecticant ranks was needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The local half-defect estimate]\label{prop:local}
For every nonzero quintic Chow term and every quotient \(P\) of its
actual factor span,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local}
 \boxed{\rank\beta+\frac{10-u}{2}\ge2d.}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For \(d=0\) this is immediate, since \(u\le10\).
If \(\ell=5\), use Lemma~\ref{lem:independent}.
Suppose \(\ell\le4\) and \(d>0\). If \(k=\ell-d\le2\),
Lemmas~\ref{lem:kernel} and \ref{lem:floor} give
\begin{align*}
 \rank\beta+\frac{10-u}{2}-2d
 &\ge5+\frac{u}{2}-\binom{k+2}{3}-2(\ell-k)\\
 &\ge\frac{(\ell-4)(\ell-5)+2}{4}
       +2k-\binom{k+2}{3}\ge0.
\end{align*}
Indeed \((\ell-4)(\ell-5)\ge0\) for \(1\le\ell\le4\), and
\(2k-\binom{k+2}{3}\) is respectively \(0,1,0\) for \(k=0,1,2\).
The only remaining possibility is \(\ell=4,d=1,k=3\), covered by
Lemma~\ref{lem:four}. Thus no integer-interval or partition table is
required to complete the cases.
\end{proof}

\section{The global dimension contradiction}
\begin{lemma}[Rectangular image spans]\label{lem:rectangular}
For linear maps \(A_i:X^*\to Y\) of ranks \(u_i\), put
\(D_Y=\dim\sum_i\im A_i\) and \(D_X=\dim\sum_i\im A_i^*\).
Then
\[
 \rank\Bigl(\sum_iA_i\Bigr)\ge D_Y+D_X-\sum_i u_i.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Factor \(A_i=B_iC_i\) through a vector space of dimension \(u_i\),
with \(B_i\) injective and \(C_i\) surjective. The concatenated maps
\(B:\bigoplus_i\K^{u_i}\to Y\) and
\(C:X^*\to\bigoplus_i\K^{u_i}\) have ranks \(D_Y,D_X\), respectively,
and \(BC=\sum_iA_i\). Here \(\im A_i^*=\im C_i^*\), since
\(B_i^*\) is onto, justifying the rank of \(C\). Since
\(\dim(\im C\cap\ker B)\le\dim\ker B=\sum_i u_i-D_Y\), the
rank of \(BC\) is at least \(D_X+D_Y-\sum_i u_i\).
\end{proof}

Suppose an actual Chow decomposition \(f=\sum_{i=1}^N T_i\) is given.
Let
\[
 F_i=\cD_2(T_i),\quad U_i=\cD_3(T_i),\quad
 u_i=\dim F_i=\dim U_i,\quad \Delta=\sum_i(10-u_i)\ge0,
\]
\[
 F=\sum_iF_i,\quad U=\sum_iU_i,\qquad
 \dim F=100+h_2,\quad\dim U=100+h_3.
\]
Linearity of catalecticants gives \(E_2\subset F\), \(E_3\subset U\),
so \(h_2,h_3\ge0\). Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:rectangular} to
\(A_i=C_{3,2}(T_i)\). Their adjoints have images \(U_i\), and their
sum \(C_{3,2}(f)\) has rank one hundred. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper}
 h_2+h_3\le10N-100-\Delta.
\end{equation}

Let \(q:F\to F/E_2\) and use polarization to define
\[
 b:U\longrightarrow V\otimes(F/E_2),\qquad b(g)=(1_V\otimes q)\delta(g).
\]
Since \(\dd U\subset F\), this map is well defined. Its kernel is
\(U\cap E_2^{(1)}=E_3\), by Lemma~\ref{lem:permanent} and
\(E_3\subset U\). Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:image-h3}
 \rank b=h_3.
\end{equation}

Order the terms arbitrarily. Put
\(L_i=\Span\{\text{factors of }T_i\}\),
\(W_i=L_1+\cdots+L_i\), \(W_0=0\), and
\(d_i=\dim(W_i/W_{i-1})\). We have \(W_N=V\), because
\(V=\dd E_2\subset\dd F\subset\sum_iL_i\). Thus \(\sum_i d_i=25\).
Set \(Z_i=b(U_i)\) and \(H_i=Z_1+\cdots+Z_i\).
As \(Z_j\subset L_j\otimes(F/E_2)\), projection to
\((W_i/W_{i-1})\otimes(F/E_2)\) kills \(H_{i-1}\). It follows that
\[
 \dim H_i-\dim H_{i-1}\ge
 \rank\bigl((P_i\otimes q|_{F_i})\delta|_{U_i}\bigr),
\]
where \(P_i:L_i\to L_i/(L_i\cap W_{i-1})\) has rank \(d_i\).
The natural map \(F_i/(F_i\cap E_2)\hookrightarrow F/E_2\) is
injective. Thus the rank on the right is exactly the local symbol rank
in Proposition~\ref{prop:local}, not an assumed independence of the
unprojected images \(Z_i\). Since \(H_N=b(U)\), summation yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower}
 h_3\ge\sum_i\left(2d_i-\frac{10-u_i}{2}\right)
      =50-\frac{\Delta}{2}.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:upper} and \eqref{eq:lower}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal}
 \boxed{h_2+\frac{\Delta}{2}\le10N-150.}
\end{equation}
For \(N<15\) the right side is negative, impossible. If \(N=15\),
nonnegativity forces \(h_2=\Delta=0\). Then \(F=E_2\), and every
\(u_i=10\), because each \(10-u_i\) is nonnegative. Consequently
\[
 F_i\subset E_2\cap\Sym^2L_i,\qquad \dim F_i=10,
\]
contradicting \eqref{eq:intersection}, since \(\dim L_i\le5\).
We conclude that \(N\ge16\).

\section{The upper bound and the logical boundary}
Glynn's identity \cite{Glynn2010} specializes to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glynn}
 \perm_5=\frac1{16}
 \sum_{\substack{\epsilon\in\{\pm1\}^5\\\epsilon_1=1}}
 \left(\prod_{r=1}^5\epsilon_r\right)
 \prod_{c=1}^5\left(\sum_{r=1}^5\epsilon_rx_{rc}\right).
\end{equation}
Here is a direct coefficient proof. Every expansion monomial chooses a
row in each of the five columns. Let \(m_r\) count its row-\(r\)
choices. The coefficient of this monomial is
\[
 \frac1{16}\prod_{r=2}^5\sum_{\epsilon_r=\pm1}\epsilon_r^{m_r+1}.
\]
It vanishes unless \(m_2,m_3,m_4,m_5\) are all odd. They are
nonnegative integers whose total, together with \(m_1\), is five.
The four odd counts must each be one, since making one at least three
would require total at least six. Then \(m_1=1\). Precisely the
permutation monomials survive, each with coefficient one. Equation
\eqref{eq:glynn} has sixteen Chow terms and proves the upper bound.
Together with the preceding section it proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}
over algebraically closed characteristic-zero fields. For any
characteristic-zero field, scalar extension preserves a hypothetical
shorter decomposition, giving the same lower bound over that field;
the rational upper bound already holds there.
